Why this exists

My SSD's own manufacturer checked the drive and told me it was fine.

It was not fine. Here is what that verdict left out.

unrecoverable read errors

0%

of its spare blocks already used

Every SSD keeps replacement blocks for when parts of it fail. Nearly a quarter of mine were already spent.

And it had done that at zero percent wear — barely used. The drive was not worn out. It was breaking.

And it was not just the software

I took the machine into Micro Center. Their techs ran OCCT — hours of stress testing across CPU, RAM, GPU and power supply — and it came back clean. Same verdict: healthy. Then they offered me an extended warranty on my own parts.

OCCT is a good tool. It just does not test the drive. It hammers compute and power hunting for instability under load; it never asks what the SSD has been logging about itself. Clean result, wrong question.

I turned the warranty down. Buying coverage on a drive I had already watched fail would have turned their miss into a free NVMe at their expense. It was the easy move. It was not the honest one.

Two opinions, one machine, one verdict — and it was wrong both times.

So why did it say Good?

Because vendor tools report a pass/fail flag, and nothing had crossed its threshold yet. Technically correct. Practically useless — the counters underneath had been climbing for weeks, and it never mentioned them.

Canary reads those counters and tells you what they mean. That is the whole idea.

What it does

Hardware, or software?

It scans for real faults — failing drives, crash patterns, driver problems — and turns Event Viewer codes into plain English. It tunes settings too, but that is the smaller half.

It matters because hardware costs money and software does not. A failing drive stalling a game load shows up as a graphics driver crash — and people buy a new GPU to fix a dying SSD.

If you think you have been hacked

Before you wipe
the whole machine.

Reinstalling Windows costs you a day and everything not backed up. Not reinstalling, when something really is on there, costs more. Canary looks for the specific ways malware stays on a PC, so the decision is based on something.

Persistence

WMI event subscriptions, sign-in hijacks, launch hijacks, injected DLLs and scheduled tasks running hidden or encoded commands.

Antivirus posture

Real-time protection and tamper protection switched off, stale definitions, and scan exclusions - a favourite trick is to exclude a folder, then put the payload in it.

Network and access

Hosts-file entries blocking security or update domains, an unexpected proxy, Remote Desktop enabled, and remote-access tools like AnyDesk or TeamViewer running.

Accounts and logs

Administrator accounts you did not create, recent password changes, Defender detection history, and whether the Security log has been cleared.

Network exposure

The same surface an attacker scans first - SMBv1 / EternalBlue, exposed Remote Desktop, open ports, missing account lockout, failed-login spikes - checked from the inside, before anyone tries.

What it cannot do

It can raise suspicion. It cannot tell you a PC is clean. Everything it does runs as an ordinary program, and anything operating at kernel level hides from all of it.

So findings mean something. An absence of findings does not. If you have real reason to think you were hacked, reinstall — and this scan should not talk you out of it.

It also tells you what to do first

Change your passwords from a different device, email first. Almost everyone gets this wrong — changing them on the compromised machine just hands over the new ones.

Then two-factor, sign out other sessions, offline scan, back up documents only, and reinstall from media made on another PC.

The part that matters

What it won't do.

“PC optimizer” has a deserved reputation. Here is what is missing on purpose — so you can judge any tool that offers it. Including this one.

Clean your registry

It is an indexed database. Unused keys cost nothing. Microsoft does not endorse cleaners, and deleting a live key breaks things weeks later.

"Free up" your RAM

Unused RAM is wasted RAM. Windows caches on purpose. Emptying it makes the next few minutes slower, not faster.

Disable services in bulk

Most sit idle using no CPU. The ones that matter — Update, Defender, audio — break in ways you will never trace back.

Promise "30% faster"

A number with no methodology is marketing. Be sceptical of any figure a tool will not show its working for.

Every change says what it does and why, records the old value, and writes an undo script. If a change cannot be explained in one sentence, it should not ship — and you should not trust a tool that will not explain it.

Why you

Tested on
exactly one PC.

Mine. An i9-13900K with an RX 7900 XTX — representative of nothing. Every check was written against one machine's quirks. No idea what it does on an NVIDIA card, an AMD CPU, a laptop, or a clean install.

It caught a real failing SSD. It also threw two false alarms I only spotted because I already knew the answer. That is what other machines will shake out.

The rule

“Could not verify”
is not “passed”.

When a check cannot run — Windows refuses the query, the tool lacks permission, the data is simply not there — Canary says so and names the gap.

It never reports a check it could not complete as one that passed. Confident false reassurance is the worst thing a diagnostic tool can do, and it is the exact failure that made this necessary.
